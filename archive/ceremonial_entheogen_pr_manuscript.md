# Ceremonial Entheogen Use in Puerto Rico: A Descriptive Characterization of Participants and Facilitators in a Non-Probability Cross-Sectional Survey 

**Manuscript-stage draft — not for circulation** 

## Structured Abstract 

**Background.** Ceremonial entheogen use occurs in Puerto Rico outside formal clinical and regulatory structures, and the practices and settings surrounding it have not been described. This study characterizes a self-selected sample of participants and facilitators, without generalizing beyond the respondents surveyed. 

**Methods.** A cross-sectional, unweighted, non-probability survey collected 100 rows; after excluding 13 abandoned blank submissions, 87 eligible adults reaching the role fork formed the analytic sample. Respondents self-identified as participants (n = 72), facilitators (n = 8), or both (n = 7); the branches are unlinked, with no identifier joining them. Tests were nonparametric with rank-biserial and epsilon-squared effect sizes; cells with n < 5 were suppressed; secondary tests were Benjamini–Hochberg corrected within family. 

**Results.** Respondents were middle-aged (n = 86; mean 42.4 years), predominantly university-educated, and almost all resident in Puerto Rico. Most recent ceremonies were single events (48 of 65 answering) in natural settings (37 of 67 answering), and satisfaction was ceiling-weighted (median 6 of 6; n = 63 answering). Five of 67 participants answering reported non-consensual physical contact (7.5%, 95% CI 3.2–16.3). An exploratory comparison of any preparation (n = 58) versus none (n = 5) on satisfaction was directionally positive ( _U_ = 218.0, p = 0.041, r_rb = 0.50, CLES = 0.75) but rests on a no-preparation group at the suppression floor and is suggestive only. 

**Conclusions.** This sample describes an educated, largely Puerto Rico–resident group reporting predominantly positive ceremonies alongside a minority reporting non-consensual contact. No prevalence, population, or causal inference is supported.

## 1. Introduction 

Ceremonial use of entheogenic substances — psilocybin-containing mushrooms, ayahuasca, and related preparations administered in structured group or guided settings — has expanded in visibility across the Americas over the past two decades, largely outside the clinical trial infrastructure that has driven the contemporary research literature [CITATION NEEDED — growth of non-clinical ceremonial and retreat-based psychedelic use]. The empirical base for this activity remains asymmetric: randomized clinical research characterizes carefully screened participants receiving standardized doses under monitored conditions, while community ceremonial practice is described mainly through ethnographic and qualitative work [CITATION NEEDED — clinical versus naturalistic psychedelic research literatures]. 

Puerto Rico occupies an unusual position in this landscape. It is a Caribbean jurisdiction under United States federal drug law, with a distinct linguistic, cultural, and health-system context, and with documented traditions of plant-based and spiritually framed healing practice [CITATION NEEDED — Puerto Rico legal status and controlled-substance framework]; [CITATION NEEDED — traditional and spiritual healing practices in Puerto Rico]. No validated Spanish-language instrument for characterizing ceremonial entheogen use in this setting was identified during study development, and the survey used here was purpose-built for that reason. The consequence is that basic descriptive questions — who takes part, what substances are used, where ceremonies occur, how long they last, whether participants are screened or prepared beforehand, and what adverse experiences are reported — have no published local answer [CITATION NEEDED — absence of prior Puerto Rico–specific survey evidence]. 

A descriptive characterization contributes in three ways. First, it establishes whether the practices reported in a community sample resemble the harm-reduction structures assumed by clinical and retreat-based frameworks — preparation, pre-ceremony screening, a stated plan for difficult experiences [CITATION NEEDED — harm-reduction and screening frameworks in psychedelic settings]. Second, it identifies which safety outcomes occur at frequencies that would justify measurement in a larger, better-powered study. Third, it produces the instrument-level evidence — item reachability, response distributions, provisional reliability — needed before any confirmatory study is designed in this population. 

The study objectives, as stated in the source analysis, were to describe the sample and the ceremonial context (Objectives 1–2) and to examine bivariate associations between preparation and reported ceremony outcomes and between participant-observable facilitator practices and perceived safety (Objectives 3–4, as revised during analysis). A safety and adverse-event domain, absent from the original proposal, was added on the recommendation of the analysis team. Every objective is descriptive. The design supports no statement about how common these practices are in Puerto Rico, and no statement about what any practice produces. 

## 2. Methods 

### 2.1 Design 

The study was a single-timepoint, cross-sectional, self-administered survey of adults reporting involvement in ceremonial entheogen use. The analysis was unweighted and the sample was non-probability. The source analysis states this constraint at the outset and it is retained here: the report makes no causal or population-prevalence claims, and neither does this manuscript. 

### 2.2 Recruitment and sample construction 

Recruitment procedures, sampling frame, fielding period, and consent process are [not reported in source]. One hundred rows were collected, meeting a target of N = 100. 

Sample construction proceeded in three stages. Of the 100 rows collected, 13 were abandoned blank submissions and were excluded. The remaining 87 eligible adults who reached the role fork constitute the descriptive analytic sample. This sample deliberately retains 14 respondents who answered "no" to both role questions and reported no ceremony participation; they were kept per protocol in order to describe who screens out, and they are excluded from all role-specific analyses. 

Respondents then split by self-identified role. Seventy-two respondents identified as participants and 8 as facilitators; 7 respondents identified as both. Role composition against the full 100 rows collected was: participant only, 65 (65%); screen-out (no/no), 14 (14%); abandoned blank, 13 (13%); dual role, 7 (7%); and facilitator only, a small number of respondents whose exact count is suppressed. The source is explicit that the roles are not a partition — dual-role respondents appear in both role-specific analyses, per protocol. 

### 2.3 Instrument and variable definitions 

The instrument was purpose-built for this study because no validated Puerto Rico–specific entheogen-use instrument exists; the analysis therefore constitutes the first reliability evidence for it. Items were fielded in Spanish and English, and open-text answers were mixed-language. 

The primary outcome, ceremony_good , recorded satisfaction with the most recent ceremony on a 0–6 scale (0 = very unpleasant to 6 = very pleasant) and was treated as ordinal. The primary exposure, prep_part , recorded preparation before the ceremony at three levels (yes, sometimes, no); a binary derivative, prep_any , contrasted any preparation against none. Secondary participant-side measures included comfort_safe (in-ceremony comfort/safety), trust_level (trust in the facilitator, 0–4), facil_background (prior knowledge of the facilitator's background or training), screening_quest (whether the facilitator asked screening 

questions beforehand), non_con_contact (non-consensual physical contact), and med_couns_part (whether the respondent spoke to a health professional beforehand). Three distinct substance variables were maintained and not merged: facilitator-led, participant across ceremonies attended, and participant most-recent ceremony. Facilitator-branch items — training_facilitator , safe_ceremony , emergency_plan , protocol_bad_exp , and exp_crisis — were facilitator self-report at a base of n = 8. 

### 2.4 Missing data, structural skips, and suppression 

Denominators shift across items for two distinct reasons, which the source distinguishes consistently: structural skips arise from branch logic and are reported as their own row rather than folded into nonresponse, while item nonresponse is the residual. Every figure in this manuscript therefore carries its own base, and no denominator is carried across sections. "Prefer not to answer" and "I'm not sure" responses were retained as substantive categories in all frequency tables but excluded from ordinal numeric tests, on the stated grounds that they carry no rank position; in composite indices they were treated as item-missing rather than scored as zero. Any subgroup cell containing fewer than five respondents was suppressed; suppressed cells are reported here qualitatively and are not back-calculated. 

### 2.5 Statistical approach 

The outcome distribution was strongly ceiling-weighted and several exposures had thin or unbalanced categories, so all tests were nonparametric: Mann–Whitney _U_ for two-group ordinal comparisons, Kruskal–Wallis _H_ for three or more groups, permutation χ² with 10,000 label permutations (seed 20260715) for thin contingency tables, and Fisher's exact test for 2×2 tables at very small n. Effect sizes were rank-biserial r_rb and common-language effect size, epsilon-squared ε², and Cramér's _V_ respectively; Wilson 95% confidence intervals were used for prevalences. 

The primary preparation comparison was pre-declared and is reported uncorrected. All other bivariate tests were assigned to prespecified secondary/exploratory families — bivariate, substance-stratified, safety-domain, and legal-knowledge — and corrected jointly within family by the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure. Families were kept separate because they share neither exposures nor outcome domains. One multivariable proportional-odds model was fitted as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating supplement; its power caveat is reported alongside it. 

### 2.6 Protocol deviations 

Three deviations from the original proposal are reported transparently. 

First, and most consequentially, the original proposal specified a four-arm preparation × integration design. The source states plainly that this design **is not estimable from the collected data** , for two stated reasons: the instrument contains no clean participant 

integration-received item, and the preparation split is badly unbalanced. Per protocol, the analysis instead examines preparation against the outcome directly. No integration measure exists anywhere in the analysis, and none is reconstructed here. 

Second, the proposal specified a perceived-safety analysis crossing facilitator training and protocol items against participant-perceived safety. Because those items are facilitator self-report (n = 8) from a different and unlinked set of respondents, with no ceremony or facilitator identifier connecting a participant to the facilitator who ran their ceremony, crossing them would have required falsely pairing unrelated respondents. The analysis substituted two participant-answerable proxies — facil_background and screening_quest — and reported facilitator self-report separately and descriptively. 

Third, the proposal specified a composite harm-reduction score mixing facilitator-side and participant-side items, which for the same reason could not validly be built. Two branch-specific composites were constructed instead: a participant-side index ( prep_any + facil_background + screening_quest ) and a facilitator-side descriptive composite. 

A related instrument limitation is reported as a data-quality finding rather than a deviation: the item no_comfort_safe ("why did you feel uncomfortable/unsafe?") received zero responses because its skip-logic condition required a single variable to equal two different values simultaneously, rendering it structurally unreachable. 

## 3. Results 

### 3.1 Who responded 

The analytic sample of 87 eligible adults skewed middle-aged and educated. Among the 86 respondents with a valid age, the mean was 42.4 years and the median 41 years (SD 12.2, range 21–75, IQR 34–50). Of the 85 respondents answering the gender item, 41 (48%) identified as women and 38 (45%) as men; a small number identified as non-binary and a small number preferred not to answer, and both cells are suppressed. 

Educational attainment was high: of the 86 answering, 41 (48%) reported a bachelor's degree, 22 (26%) a master's, 10 (12%) an associate's degree, and 7 (8%) a high school diploma or GED, with doctorate holders and prefer-not-to-answer both suppressed. Self-rated economic situation was correspondingly favorable: of the 86 answering, 49 (57%) described it as comfortable, 26 (30%) as tight, and 8 (9%) as difficult. 

Residency was concentrated but not exclusive. Eighty-two respondents reported residing in Puerto Rico, representing 95% of those answering; a small number reported residence in the 

United States and a small number in Spain, both suppressed. The source treats residency as a descriptive covariate rather than an inclusion filter, and notes explicitly that the presence of non-Puerto Rico respondents tempers any reading of the sample as describing Puerto Rico specifically. 

Role composition is described in Methods. The structural feature that governs everything below is that the participant branch (n = 72) and the facilitator branch (n = 8) are different, unlinked sets of respondents, overlapping only in the 7 dual-role respondents, with no identifier connecting any participant to the facilitator who ran their ceremony. 

Free-text engagement was substantial relative to sample size: 37 open-text fields yielded 285 non-empty answers, of which 220 (77%) were classified as Spanish, 54 (19%) as English, and 11 (4%) as mixed or other by a heuristic the source describes as characterizing the corpus rather than validly classifying each item. The most-answered fields were an experience-highlight item (58 answers), an opinion-on-safety item (54), and a preparation-guidance description (45). 

### 3.2 The ceremonial context 

Participants reported extensive but not recent ceremonial histories. Among the 67 participants answering, the median number of lifetime ceremonies attended was 6 (mean 15.1, SD 35.7, range 1–250, IQR 3–12) — a long right tail that makes the median the appropriate summary — while ceremonies in the past 12 months were far fewer among the same 67 answering (median 1, mean 1.3, SD 1.7, range 0–8, IQR 0–2). Consistent with this, of the 66 answering the time-since-last-ceremony item, 30 (45%) reported more than 12 months ago, 20 (30%) reported 2 to 6 months ago, and 11 (17%) reported 7 to 12 months ago, with the past-month and prefer-not-to-answer cells suppressed. 

Substances were reported through three variables the source keeps separate. For substances used across ceremonies attended, participant reports ranked magic mushrooms first, followed by ayahuasca, tobacco, cacao, DMT, LSD, MDMA, and ketamine; the source presents this as a figure without numeric labels, so exact counts per substance are [not reported in source]. For the most recent ceremony specifically, the grouping used in stratified analyses gave individually reportable groups of ayahuasca (n = 23) and magic mushrooms (n = 31), with cacao, DMT, tobacco, LSD, and "other" each individually below the suppression floor and pooled into a combined other/rare group (n = 11) under a prespecified rule; a single "prefer not to answer" response was excluded as missing exposure. 

The most recent ceremony was typically a discrete, day-length, outdoor event. Of the 65 answering the format item, 48 (74%) reported a single ceremony rather than a retreat or series and 13 (20%) reported a retreat or series, with the remaining categories suppressed. Of the 67 answering the place item, 37 (55%) reported a natural setting such as a forest, beach, or mountain, 15 (22%) a private home, and 9 (13%) a retreat center or wellness facility, with remaining categories suppressed. Duration was concentrated in the multi-hour range: of the 65 

answering, 19 (29%) reported 4 to 6 hours, 19 (29%) overnight (approximately 12 or more hours), 14 (22%) 7 to 12 hours, and 7 (11%) 1 to 3 hours, with the multi-day and under-one-hour categories suppressed. 

### 3.3 Preparation, screening, and what participants knew beforehand 

Preparation was near-universal among those answering: the three-level preparation variable distributed as 52 respondents reporting preparation, 6 reporting occasional preparation, and 5 reporting none. This imbalance is why the four-arm design was abandoned, and it constrains every preparation analysis below. 

Participant-observable facilitator practices were common but not uniform. Fifty-four participants reported knowing about the facilitator's background or training beforehand, 8 reported knowing a little, and a suppressed number reported not knowing. On screening, 39 reported that the facilitator asked screening questions beforehand, 15 reported that none were asked, and 7 were not sure — a descriptive finding of the sample rather than an estimate of any rate. 

Disclosure outside the ceremonial context was uncommon. Across the legal-knowledge comparisons, 7 to 8 respondents reported having spoken to a doctor, therapist, or other health professional about their ceremony participation beforehand, against 47 to 50 who reported not having done so, with the not-sure cell suppressed; denominators differ by predictor because each comparison uses its own pairwise-complete base. 

The two participant-side legal-knowledge items, harmonized onto a shared 0–4 code, correlated strongly (Spearman ρ = 0.79, p = 0.000; Cronbach's α = 0.86, 95% CI 0.75–0.92, complete-case n = 55). Across the nine comparisons of three legal predictors against three participant-side outcomes, only most-recent-ceremony legal worry differed by disclosure to a health professional at the uncorrected level ( _H_ = 6.92, p = 0.031, ε² = 0.09; median 2, IQR 0–2 among the 8 reporting disclosure versus median 0, IQR 0–0 among the 47 reporting none), and this did not survive correction (q = 0.283). 

### 3.4 Experience and perceived safety 

Reported satisfaction with the most recent ceremony was overwhelmingly positive. Of the 72 participants, 63 answered ceremony_good ; the remainder were item nonresponse, with structural skips affecting non-participants excluded from this base. The distribution was strongly ceiling-weighted, with a median of 6 and the mode at the scale maximum — a ceiling that constrains the discriminating power of every analysis using this outcome. 

Perceived in-ceremony comfort and safety was similarly high and similarly undifferentiated, returning a median of 3 in every subgroup tested and differing neither by prior knowledge of the facilitator's background (54 "yes", 8 "a little", "no" suppressed; _H_ = 2.24, p = 0.326, ε² = 0.00) nor by whether screening questions were asked (39 "yes", 15 "no", 7 "not sure"; _H_ = 2.40, p = 0.301, ε² = 0.01). 

Trust in the facilitator was the one participant-side comparison that differed at the uncorrected level. Among the 54 who reported knowing the facilitator's background beforehand the median trust score was 4 (IQR 3–4), against 3 (IQR 2–3) among the 8 who knew a little and a suppressed group reporting no prior knowledge ( _H_ = 8.15, p = 0.017, ε² = 0.10). Because a contributing cell falls below the suppression floor the source designates this descriptive and exploratory only, and it did not survive correction (q = 0.102). Trust did not differ by screening status (39, 15, and 7 respondents, all median 4; _H_ = 0.77, p = 0.679, ε² = −0.02). 

Satisfaction did not differ significantly by most-recent-ceremony substance group: median 5 (IQR 4–6) among the 22 ayahuasca respondents answering the outcome, 6 (IQR 5–6) among the 30 magic mushroom respondents, and 6 (IQR 4–6) among the 11 in the combined other/rare group ( _H_ = 4.98, p = 0.083, ε² = 0.05; q = 0.166). 

The safety domain, added on the analysis team's recommendation and absent from the original proposal, produced the sample's most consequential descriptive finding. Of the 67 participants answering the non-consensual physical contact item, 61 (91%) reported no such contact and 5 (7%) reported that it occurred, with a small not-sure group suppressed; the reported prevalence within this sample was 7.5% (95% CI 3.2–16.3; k = 5, n = 67). This figure describes these 67 respondents and nothing beyond them. 

Cross-tabulations of non-consensual contact against the two participant-side proxies are reported by the source under an explicit disclosure note: sensitive items are otherwise reported aggregate-only, and the research team elected to publish these cross-tabs under the standard suppression convention rather than withhold them, while cautioning that several masked cells remain close to case level given that affirmative or uncertain reports total 6 of the 67 answering. Under that caution, non-consensual contact was associated with prior knowledge of the facilitator's background at the uncorrected level (permutation χ² = 18.90, p = 0.047, Cramér's _V_ = 0.38, pairwise-complete n = 67) but not with screening (permutation χ² = 13.64, p = 0.094, _V_ = 0.32, n = 66); neither survived correction (both q = 0.236), and neither did the comparison by substance group (permutation χ² = 3.70, p = 0.486, _V_ = 0.17, n = 65; q = 0.486). The source directs that every result in this subsection be read as hypothesis-generating and as inadequately powered either to confirm or to rule out an association. 

One instrument-level observation belongs here because it bears on outcome measurement: an item on perceived positive life impact had zero variance, with all 67 respondents answering selecting the same option. The source reports this as a data-quality finding rather than a scale result. 

### 3.5 Facilitator practice — a separate, non-generalizable subsample (n = 8) 

**The material in this subsection comes from facilitator self-report at a base of n = 8 and is descriptive only.** These respondents are a different, unlinked set from the participants 

described above; no facilitator here can be connected to any participant's ceremony, and nothing in this subsection is compared against or read alongside participant-reported experience. Nearly every category cell sits at or below the suppression floor, and the source states that no group comparison at this base is meaningful. 

Preparation for the facilitator role was informal. Six of the 8 facilitators (75%) reported personal experience and self-study; mentorship or apprenticeship learning and family or ancestral tradition were each reported by suppressed numbers. No facilitator reported formal certifications or academic courses or workshops — both categories were zero. Of the 7 answering, 6 (86%) reported giving participants preparation instructions, and 6 (86%) reported typically facilitating in an outdoor space or near nature; dosing approaches were dispersed across four categories, every one of them suppressed at that base. 

On screening, 6 of 8 (75%) reported having an initial conversation or interview with the person, with questions about medical history, current medication, and mental health each reported by suppressed numbers. On responding to a difficult experience, 5 of 8 (62%) reported singing, playing music, or using sound to guide the person, with the remaining approaches suppressed. On emergency planning, all responses at a base of 7 answering fell into informal or conditional categories — no facilitator selected the formal option — and both categories are suppressed. A facilitator-side composite readiness score (range 0–4) placed 6 of the 7 complete cases (86%) at 3.5, with the remainder suppressed; the source notes this branch is close to constant, so neither a reliability statistic nor an association test is meaningful at this base. 

Facilitator-witnessed crisis signs were reported by 7 facilitators answering. Individual crisis sub-items, including suicidal ideation and panic attack, are each suppressed; the source flags them only to establish that serious crisis events were witnessed and fall within scope, and states that no stable rate is estimable. Association tests between witnessed crisis and 

facilitator-reported protocol items — the one place in the study where both variables sit on the same side of the survey and no linkage gap exists — returned undefined odds ratios at n = 7 (Fisher's exact p = 0.143 against distress-response protocol, q = 0.238; p = 0.429 against emergency plan, q = 0.536), and two further tests could not be run for insufficient non-degenerate data. The source states that every 2×2 table here has at least one margin below 2, that power is essentially nil, and that these results establish an analytic approach for a future larger facilitator sample rather than constituting evidence for or against an association. 

### 3.6 Preparation and reported satisfaction — an exploratory comparison 

**This comparison is reported as suggestive and exploratory, not confirmatory.** Its structural weakness is stated before its result: the no-preparation group contains 5 respondents answering the outcome, which the source describes as sitting at the suppression floor. 

Among the 63 participants answering ceremony_good , 58 reported some preparation (median 6, IQR 5–6) and 5 reported none (median 4, IQR 3–5). The Mann–Whitney comparison returned 

_U_ = 218.0, p = 0.041, rank-biserial r_rb = 0.50, common-language effect size CLES = 0.75, with preparation associated with higher reported satisfaction. The source's own interpretation is unambiguous and is preserved: even a sizable effect at this base is statistically fragile, and the finding should be reported as suggestive and exploratory rather than confirmatory. It is not a headline result. 

The prespecified sensitivity analysis weakens it further. Across the three preparation levels (52, 6, and 5 respondents), the Kruskal–Wallis test returned _H_ = 4.24, p = 0.120, ε² = 0.04 — non-significant, with two of the three cells below the suppression floor once the outcome is restricted to non-missing values, which the source designates descriptive only. Within the secondary/exploratory family, the three-level comparison carried q = 0.359. 

A participant-side harm-reduction practice index (preparation, prior knowledge of facilitator background, and being screened; range 0–3) served as the valid within-branch replacement for the proposal's mixed composite. Of the 54 participants with complete data on all three items, 35 (65%) reported all three practices and 16 (30%) reported two, with the remaining cell suppressed. Internal consistency was low (Cronbach's α = 0.14, 95% CI −0.36–0.47, n = 54, 3 items); the source concludes that the items capture distinct protective practices rather than one latent construct and directs that the score be read as a count of practices present, not a validated scale. The index was not associated with satisfaction ( _H_ = 2.09, p = 0.352, ε² = 0.00; medians 4, 5, and 6 across scores of 1, 2, and 3, score-1 suppressed once the outcome is non-missing; q = 0.422), nor with non-consensual contact when binarized (permutation χ² = 0.56, p = 0.641, _V_ = 0.10, n = 54; q = 0.641). 

An exploratory proportional-odds model was fitted as a hypothesis-generating supplement, adjusting preparation for age and facilitator trust simultaneously. The proposal's full covariate set was not estimable — gender cells were too thin for a four-level predictor, and screening overlapped substantively with the trust and background measures already in the model — so a prespecified three-predictor fallback was used, with the outcome collapsed to three ordered levels for this model only (all other tables retain the full 0–6 scale). In the 62 complete cases (of 63 with a non-missing outcome; 9 low/mixed, 19 mostly positive, 34 very positive), trust in the facilitator was associated with higher satisfaction (OR 2.20, 95% CI 1.06–4.53, p = 0.033), while any preparation did not reach significance (OR 5.21, 95% CI 0.84–32.41, p = 0.077) and age was flat (OR 0.99, 95% CI 0.94–1.03, p = 0.527); omnibus likelihood-ratio χ²(3) = 8.68, p = 0.034, McFadden pseudo-R² = 0.07. The source attaches an explicit power caveat: roughly 12 cases per estimated parameter, below conventional rules of thumb; the proportional-odds assumption was assumed rather than tested; and no estimate should be presented without that caveat. 

## 4. Discussion 

### 4.1 A structurally informal practice landscape 

The most stable picture this sample supports is a practice organized around informal, relationally transmitted expertise rather than credentialed or documented protocol. Among the 8 facilitators who self-reported — a subsample too small to generalize from, and one that cannot be connected to any participant here — training came from personal experience, self-study, mentorship, and family tradition; no one reported formal certification or academic coursework, and emergency planning was uniformly informal or conditional. On the participant side the informality reads differently: preparation was near-universal among those answering, but roughly a quarter of participants answering the screening item reported that no screening questions were asked, and a further group was uncertain. 

Two features are worth separating. Preparation is something participants largely do, whether self-directed or instructed; screening must be done _to_ them, and was substantially less consistent in what these respondents reported. That asymmetry, if it holds in a larger sample, would locate the intervention target on the facilitator side — but this sample cannot establish whether it holds, and the facilitator branch is far too small to characterize screening practice. 

### 4.2 A ceiling that limits what satisfaction can reveal 

Reported satisfaction clustered at the top of its scale: a median of 6 on a 0–6 scale among the 63 participants answering, with the mode at the maximum. This is a substantive finding in its own right — these respondents overwhelmingly described their most recent ceremony positively — but it also constrains the analysis severely. An outcome with almost no variance in the lower range cannot discriminate between groups, and the pattern of null results across the secondary family is consistent with that constraint rather than with the absence of any association. The same ceiling appeared elsewhere: a perceived-life-impact item had zero variance, and the participant-side comfort/safety measure returned a median of 3 in every subgroup tested. The design implication is that satisfaction, measured this way, is close to exhausted as an outcome for this population; future work would need measures with headroom in both directions before group comparisons on experience quality could be informative. 

### 4.3 Safety signals that the design can identify but not quantify 

Five of 67 participants answering reported non-consensual physical contact (7.5%, 95% CI 3.2–16.3 within this sample), and facilitators reported having witnessed crisis signs, though every individual crisis sub-item was suppressed and no rate was estimable at a base of 7. These two findings come from different, unlinked reporters and cannot be combined. 

The study establishes that these events occur among respondents and fall within what an instrument of this kind can capture. It cannot establish how often they occur, in whom, or under what conditions. The cross-tabulations against participant-side proxies are described by the source as inadequately powered either to confirm or to rule out an association, and the one comparison reaching uncorrected significance did not survive correction; treating any of them as an actionable signal would overstate them considerably. The safety domain — added by the analysis team rather than specified in the proposal — justified its inclusion by identifying a measurement priority, not by producing an estimate. 

### 4.4 The preparation finding as a design lesson 

The preparation-to-satisfaction comparison is the study's pre-declared primary analysis and its most fragile result, and those two facts should be held together rather than traded off. The direction was positive and the effect sizes were not small (r_rb = 0.50, CLES = 0.75), but the no-preparation group contained 5 respondents answering the outcome, the three-level sensitivity analysis was non-significant with two cells below the suppression floor, and the adjusted model placed preparation short of conventional significance with a confidence interval spanning nearly two orders of magnitude. The finding is suggestive and exploratory throughout, and nothing here should be read as establishing it. 

The more durable contribution is diagnostic. The preparation split was unbalanced enough to dissolve the original four-arm design, and it remains the binding constraint on the primary analysis. Any successor study intending to examine preparation will need either a sampling strategy that reaches unprepared participants in usable numbers or a question that does not depend on that contrast. Reporting the deviation rather than absorbing it quietly is what makes the constraint visible to the next design. 

## 5. Limitations 

The sample was non-probability and unweighted, and no recruitment frame is described. Nothing here supports a statement about how common ceremonial entheogen use is in Puerto Rico, about people who use entheogens in Puerto Rico generally, or about any population beyond the respondents surveyed. The concentration in higher education categories — 63 of 86 answering reported a bachelor's degree or higher — and the favorable self-rated economic distribution are consistent with self-selection into an online survey and describe who responded, not who participates. Approximately 5% of those answering the residency item reported living outside Puerto Rico, which the source notes further tempers any Puerto Rico–specific reading. 

Role subsamples were small and unevenly sized. The facilitator branch comprised 8 respondents, at which base nearly every category cell was suppressed, no rate was estimable, 

and every attempted 2×2 association test had at least one margin below 2. Facilitator findings are descriptive context only. 

Participant and facilitator respondents are unlinked. There is no ceremony or facilitator identifier connecting a participant to the facilitator who ran their ceremony. This is not a minor analytic inconvenience: it eliminated the proposal's integration-proxy comparison, its perceived-safety-by-facilitator-training analysis, and its mixed harm-reduction composite, and it means that no statement in this manuscript connects what facilitators reported doing to what participants reported experiencing. 

The outcome exhibited a pronounced ceiling. With a median of 6 on a 0–6 scale and severe thinness in the lower cells, the outcome had limited capacity to discriminate between groups, and the raw scale had to be collapsed for the exploratory ordinal model to avoid quasi-complete separation. 

The primary preparation comparison rests on a no-preparation group at the suppression floor and is exploratory throughout. Its sensitivity analysis was non-significant and its adjusted estimate did not reach conventional significance. 

All measures were single-timepoint self-report. For most respondents, the most recent ceremony was distant — 30 of 66 answering reported more than 12 months, and lifetime ceremony counts reached 250 — so recall error and reconstruction are plausible and unmeasurable here. 

No integration measure exists. The instrument contains no clean participant integration-received item, so nothing in this study speaks to post-ceremony integration. 

The instrument is unvalidated, and the analysis produced its first reliability evidence, which is mixed: a two-item trust-and-comfort pair returned Cronbach's α = 0.58 (95% CI 0.31–0.74, n = 64) alongside Spearman ρ = 0.39 (p = 0.002), a harmonized legal-knowledge pair returned α = 0.86 (95% CI 0.75–0.92, n = 55), and the participant-side practice index returned α = 0.14 (95% CI −0.36–0.47, n = 54). Two instrument logic errors were documented — a structurally unreachable item and an asymmetrically gated legal-worry item reaching only 6 respondents — and other proposed scale candidates proved untestable. 

Finally, data-quality flags remain unadjudicated: two respondents had more than one answered-but-incorrect attention item, two rows shared an identical nickname-and-password combination, 9 rows shared a repeated nickname, and 12 shared a repeated password. No duplicate-detection metadata was available, rows were flagged rather than dropped per protocol, and the source recommends a sensitivity re-run once true duplicates are confirmed. The results reported here precede that adjudication. 

## 6. Conclusion 

Within a self-selected sample of 87 adults, ceremonial entheogen use was described by a middle-aged, university-educated, overwhelmingly Puerto Rico–resident group whose most recent ceremonies were typically single, multi-hour, outdoor events involving psilocybin mushrooms or ayahuasca, and who reported those ceremonies in strongly positive terms. Preparation was near-universal while pre-ceremony screening was not, and the small facilitator subsample described training and emergency planning as informal rather than credentialed or documented. Five of 67 participants answering reported non-consensual physical contact, and facilitators reported witnessing crisis events at a base too small for any rate to be estimated. The pre-declared preparation-to-satisfaction comparison was directionally positive but rests on a no-preparation group at the suppression floor and is exploratory only. These findings describe this sample alone. They support no estimate of prevalence and no inference about what any practice produces, and their principal value lies in identifying which measures, sampling strategies, and instrument corrections a properly powered successor study would require.
